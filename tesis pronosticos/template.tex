\abstract{Demand forecasting plays a critical role in operations management, particularly in environments where production and inventory decisions depend on forward-looking estimates. However, many organizations still rely on simple averages and manual calculations, limiting forecast accuracy and systematic error evaluation. This study addresses this gap by evaluating nine forecasting methods—simple average, moving average, weighted average, simple exponential smoothing, double exponential smoothing, Holt–Winters, linear regression, ARIMA, and SARIMA—using error metrics within a walk-forward validation scheme to identify the most appropriate technique for different demand series. Based on this comparative analysis, a computational tool was developed in Python using the Dash library to automate data loading, method implementation, metric calculation, and interactive visualization of results. Additionally, the computational performance of the tool was assessed by analyzing processing times under different time series sizes. The results show that integrating multiple forecasting methods within a unified and automated environment enables systematic comparison, improves transparency, and supports more informed production and inventory decisions. The study concludes that computational tools that combine standardized evaluation metrics and interactive visualization can strengthen demand planning processes and reduce reliance on empirical forecasting practices.}

Demand planning plays a central role in operations management, particularly in environments where production, procurement, and inventory decisions depend on anticipatory information. In increasingly dynamic markets characterized by volatility, global competition, and evolving consumption patterns, forecasting future demand becomes both complex and indispensable (Mentzer et al., 2001; Chopra and Meindl, 2021). Accurate forecasting reduces uncertainty, improves resource allocation, and mitigates operational imbalances. However, demand rarely follows perfectly stable patterns. Seasonality, structural variability, irregular consumption behavior, and external shocks limit predictive precision and challenge traditional forecasting methods (Makridakis et al., 1998; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, 2018).

The importance of forecasting in logistics and inventory systems has been extensively documented. Forecasts directly influence storage decisions, transportation planning, procurement policies, and safety stock levels (Ballou, 2004). Persistent forecast errors may generate overstocks, stockouts, and service-level instability (Silver, Pyke and Peterson, 1998). Empirical studies show that improving forecast accuracy can significantly reduce total inventory costs while increasing product availability, highlighting the strong relationship between forecast accuracy and operational performance (Syntetos et al., 2009; Fildes et al., 2019). Recent research further indicates that enhanced forecasting performance may translate into measurable cost reductions and improved supply chain responsiveness (Sattar et al., 2025). 

From a methodological perspective, classical time-series models such as moving averages, exponential smoothing, regression models, and ARIMA-based approaches remain widely used due to their interpretability and ability to capture trend and seasonality components (Makridakis et al., 1998; Nahmias and Olsen, 2015; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, 2018).

Nonetheless, in environments characterized by intermittent, highly volatile, or structurally unstable demand, these traditional models may underperform. This has encouraged the integration of advanced computational tools, including machine learning algorithms and data-driven forecasting systems (Carbonneau et al., 2008; Luo, 2024).
Despite methodological advances, organizational practice often lags behind academic development. Many firms continue to rely on manual spreadsheets and empirical adjustments, limiting systematic error evaluation and reducing transparency in forecasting processes (Madariaga Fernández, 2020). Kerkkänen, Korpela, and Huiskonen (2009) report that a substantial proportion of companies fail to properly measure forecast errors, weakening the integration between forecasting and operational planning. This gap between theoretical capability and practical implementation remains a persistent challenge in supply chain management.

At the same time, the literature reflects diverging perspectives regarding the superiority of complex models over simpler statistical techniques. Large-scale forecasting competitions (e.g., the M-competitions) have shown that, in many contexts, relatively simple statistical methods perform comparably to or better than more sophisticated approaches (Makridakis et al., 2018). Conversely, other studies argue that machine learning and hybrid models outperform traditional techniques when large datasets and nonlinear patterns are present (Carbonneau et al., 2008). This ongoing debate suggests that forecasting performance is highly context-dependent and reinforces the need for systematic model comparison under homogeneous evaluation criteria.

Recent research also emphasizes that computational tools enhance decision-making quality when uncertainty is explicitly visualized and interpreted. Visual analytics approaches integrating automated statistical modeling with interactive dashboards improve user understanding and facilitate managerial adoption (Keim et al., 2010; Maack et al., 2025). In particular, uncertainty-aware visualization frameworks allow decision-makers to assess forecast variability, compare competing models, and better understand error behavior. These developments support the design of accessible computational tools capable of integrating statistical rigor with intuitive interfaces.

In this context, this study addresses the following research question: Which forecasting methods provide superior performance under varying demand structures when evaluated through consistent statistical metrics, and how can this evaluation process be automated through an interactive computational tool? To answer this question, nine forecasting methods are implemented and compared: simple average, moving average, weighted moving average, simple exponential smoothing, double exponential smoothing (Holt), Holt–Winters, linear regression, ARIMA, and SARIMA. Each method is evaluated using standardized performance metrics, including Mean Error (ME), Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), ensuring objective comparability.

The primary contribution of this study lies in the development of a generalized computational framework that integrates multiple classical forecasting methods, automated error evaluation, and interactive visualization in a single platform. The tool is developed in Python using the Dash library, enabling users without advanced statistical training to upload historical series, compare models under identical conditions, and interpret uncertainty through dynamic visualizations.
In addition to methodological comparison, computational performance is analyzed by measuring processing time as a function of series length, providing insight into scalability and practical feasibility. The principal findings indicate that forecasting accuracy varies significantly according to demand structure, that no single model universally dominates across scenarios, and that automated comparative evaluation substantially improves transparency and decision support. These results reinforce the importance of structured forecasting processes over reliance on isolated or manual techniques.

By combining statistical modeling, systematic evaluation, and interactive visualization, this study contributes to bridging the gap between forecasting theory and operational practice. The proposed framework provides a replicable and scalable approach for organizations seeking to improve demand planning robustness while maintaining methodological transparency.

Section 3 presents the empirical and computational results. Section 4 discusses findings in relation to the existing literature. Section 5 concludes with implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

\subsection{Research Design}

This study follows a quantitative and applied research design aimed at mathematically analyzing demand time series and developing a generalized computational forecasting framework suitable for real-world operational environments. The research is non-experimental, as historical demand records are analyzed without manipulation of independent variables. It is longitudinal in nature, given that monthly time-series data are evaluated over multiple periods to capture structural dynamics.
The methodological approach is descriptive and propositional. First, structural components of the time series (trend, seasonality, stationarity) are identified. Second, a computational tool is developed to automate model comparison, parameter optimization, and forecast generation. Similar applied quantitative approaches are widely adopted in demand planning and inventory forecasting research (Petropoulos et al., 2022; Makridakis et al., 2022).

\subsection{Data and Experimental Material}

The experimental material consists of monthly demand time series structured with three variables: year, month, and demand. Although empirical validation was conducted using industrial sales data, the tool is fully generalized and accepts any dataset that follows this structure.
Input files must be provided in .xlsx format with the exact column names: year, month and demand.
During data loading, the system:Converts textual month labels into numerical format,
constructs a monthly time index, validates column presence and data coherence, checks numeric consistency of demand values. If validation fails, the system returns an error message, preventing further processing. This ensures data integrity and replicability.

\subsection{Series Classification Procedure}

Before model comparison, the tool performs structural classification along three dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Trend Detection:
A simple linear regression of demand against time is estimated. Statistical significance of the slope (p < 0.05) indicates deterministic trend presence. This approach aligns with recent forecasting structure-detection practices (Talagala et al., 2021).
\item Seasonality Detection:
Autocorrelation at lag 12 is computed. Absolute autocorrelation above 0.30 indicates annual seasonality, consistent with time-series diagnostics standards (Wei, 2006; Montgomery et al., 2015).
\item Stationarity:
The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test (ADF) is applied. A p-value < 0.05 indicates stationarity.
The classification results are displayed to users but do not automatically transform the models, preserving methodological transparency.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Forecasting Methods Included}

Nine forecasting methods are implemented:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Simple Average
\item Moving Average
\item Weighted Moving Average
\item Simple Exponential Smoothing
\item Holt’s Linear Trend
\item Holt–Winters
\item Linear Regression
\item ARIMA
\item SARIMA
\end{enumerate}
Classical models follow formulations described in Nahmias and Olsen (2015) and Montgomery et al. (2015).
The inclusion of both simple statistical models and ARIMA-based approaches is consistent with findings from large-scale forecasting competitions, demonstrating that no single method dominates across all demand structures (Makridakis et al., 2022).
Recent evidence shows that systematic comparison across heterogeneous models improves robustness under demand uncertainty (Petropoulos et al., 2022).

\subsection {Validation Strategy}

Model evaluation follows a walk-forward one-step-ahead rolling origin validation scheme, widely recommended for dependent time-series data (Bergmeir et al., 2020).
For each time step t:
\begin{itemize}
\item The model is trained using data up to \begin{equation} t-1 \end{equation}
\item A one-step-ahead forecast is generated
\item The process repeats recursively across the series
\end{itemize}
This procedure prevents look-ahead bias and yields realistic performance estimates, as recommended in contemporary forecasting evaluation research (Makridakis et al., 2022).

\subsection {Error Metrics}
The following metrics are computed:
\begin{itemize}
\item Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)
\item Mean Squared Error (MSE)
\item Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)
\item Mean Error (ME)
\end{itemize}
MAPE is used for final ranking due to interpretability and comparability across scales (Makridakis et al., 2022).

\subsection {Hyperparameter Optimization}
After initial ranking, the best-performing model undergoes parameter optimization through grid search:
\begin{itemize}
\item SES: alpha sweep
\item Holt: (alpha, beta) grid
\item Holt–Winters: (alpha, beta, gamma) two-stage grid
\item ARIMA/SARIMA: compact (p,d,q) and seasonal (P,D,Q) combinations
Each parameter combination is re-evaluated using walk-forward validation.
Search ranges are computationally bounded to prevent exponential growth in runtime, consistent with scalable forecasting system design principles (Benidis et al., 2022).
\end{itemize}

\item Final forecasting and export
\end{itemize}

Based on the previous classification, Figure 4 illustrates how the tool applies internal filtering rules to exclude forecasting methods that are structurally inconsistent with the identified characteristics of the series. Since the series does not exhibit seasonality, the Holt–Winters method and SARIMA models with seasonal components were excluded from evaluation. Conversely, because the series presents a statistically significant trend, models capable of capturing systematic level changes—such as Linear Regression, ARIMA, and Holt—were retained.
This selection strategy is consistent with recent forecasting research emphasizing that model performance is highly dependent on structural time-series features and that model selection should be aligned with these characteristics rather than applied uniformly across heterogeneous data (Montero-Manso et al., 2020; Makridakis et al., 2022). Feature-based model selection approaches demonstrate that incorporating structural information—such as trend or seasonality—improves forecast robustness and reduces the risk of structural mismatch.
In this case, Linear Regression achieved the best performance, presenting the lowest MAPE and greater error stability compared with Holt and ARIMA, indicating that deterministic trend modeling was sufficient to capture the dominant demand dynamics.

For this series, Linear Regression exhibits slightly more stable errors compared with ARIMA and Holt, which show greater variability in certain months. These characteristics favor models structured around a trend component, such as Linear Regression, as opposed to more complex methods requiring additional structural patterns to improve fit, such as Holt or ARIMA.

\subsection{Comparison with Prophet}
To further examine comparative performance, forecast accuracy was analyzed as a function of training sample size. Figure X presents the MAPE values of both Prophet and the proposed tool, ordered by increasing data length, allowing a clear visualization of performance dominance across different information regimes.

The graphical analysis reveals a differentiated performance pattern. For very short series (24 observations), the proposed tool substantially outperforms Prophet, achieving a markedly lower MAPE, indicating greater robustness under data scarcity conditions. In intermediate sample sizes (60–96 observations), the tool maintains competitive performance and frequently achieves lower or comparable error levels. However, as the series length increases beyond 120 observations, Prophet tends to exhibit more stable improvements and, in several cases, achieves lower MAPE values.

These results suggest that the proposed tool demonstrates particular strength in short and moderately sized series, where structural filtering and parsimonious model selection reduce the risk of overfitting. This behavior is consistent with findings from large-scale forecasting competitions, which indicate that simpler or structurally aligned statistical models can outperform more complex approaches when data availability is limited (Makridakis et al., 2020; Makridakis et al., 2022). Conversely, as data volume increases, models designed to capture flexible nonlinear patterns—such as Prophet—may benefit from additional information, leading to improved stability.

Overall, the evidence indicates that the proposed software provides a clear advantage in low-data operational contexts, a common scenario in small and medium-sized inventory systems, while remaining competitive as data availability grows. This reinforces its suitability for real-world demand forecasting environments characterized by heterogeneous series lengths and limited historical information.
